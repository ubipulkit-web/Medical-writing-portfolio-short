# Return to Sport After ACL Reconstruction: Why Time Alone Is Not Enough

**HCP Evidence Brief | Independent medical writing portfolio sample — not commissioned or published**

**Audience:** Sports medicine physicians, physiotherapists and rehabilitation professionals

## Key Message

Return to sport (RTS) following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) should not be treated as a single calendar-based decision. Evidence supports an approach combining adequate rehabilitation time with assessment of strength, functional performance, psychological readiness and sport-specific demands.

## The Clinical Problem

Successful reconstruction does not mark the end of ACL rehabilitation.

For athletes returning to pivoting and cutting sports, the period following ACLR remains clinically important because exposure to high-intensity sport occurs before biological, neuromuscular and psychological recovery necessarily converge.

The practical challenge is therefore identifying when an athlete is sufficiently prepared to tolerate sport-specific loading.

Historically, postoperative time has frequently acted as the principal marker for progression. Although time remains important, research suggests that it cannot adequately characterise recovery when used alone.

## 1. Early Return Is Associated With Increased Reinjury Risk in Some Cohorts

In the Delaware–Oslo ACL cohort, Grindem and colleagues prospectively followed athletes participating in pivoting sports following ACL reconstruction.

Reinjury risk decreased as return to sport was delayed up to nine months after surgery. The investigators also observed substantial differences in reinjury between athletes who passed and failed their return-to-sport criteria, although the difference for the composite criteria did not reach conventional statistical significance.

A later prospective cohort of young athletes reported that participants returning to knee-strenuous sport before nine months following ACL reconstruction had a substantially higher rate of a second ACL injury than those who returned later.

These findings support caution with premature return. However, they should not be interpreted as proof that crossing the nine-month threshold automatically produces a low-risk athlete.

## 2. Time and Readiness Measure Different Things

Postoperative time provides a proxy for biological recovery and rehabilitation exposure.

It does not directly measure:

- quadriceps strength;
- hamstring function;
- movement quality;
- hop performance;
- neuromuscular control;
- psychological readiness;
- tolerance of sport-specific loading; or
- decision-making during reactive sporting tasks.

An athlete at ten months after reconstruction may therefore demonstrate substantial functional deficits, while another athlete at the same postoperative stage may have achieved considerably greater rehabilitation milestones.

Time can act as a **minimum contextual criterion**, but functional recovery requires separate evaluation.

## 3. Passing RTS Test Batteries Does Not Eliminate Risk

Criteria-based testing is clinically appealing because it provides objective data beyond postoperative time.

However, the evidence is more complex than a simple "pass equals safe" model.

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Losciale and colleagues evaluated whether passing RTS criteria was associated with reduced second ACL injury risk. Only four studies met the inclusion criteria. The pooled estimate did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in overall second ACL injury among athletes who passed RTS testing, and the certainty of evidence was very low.

This finding does not establish that RTS testing is ineffective.

Instead, it highlights limitations in existing test batteries and research, including heterogeneity between testing protocols, relatively small study populations and variation in the definition of successful clearance.

## 4. Limb Symmetry Should Be Interpreted Carefully

Limb symmetry indices are commonly incorporated into ACL rehabilitation.

A frequently used threshold is approximately 90% symmetry between the reconstructed and contralateral limb.

This approach is practical but has an important limitation: the uninjured limb is not necessarily a stable representation of the athlete's preinjury capacity.

Bilateral detraining can allow an athlete to appear symmetrical despite reduced absolute performance.

RTS interpretation should therefore consider absolute strength and performance alongside between-limb symmetry wherever normative or preinjury information is available.

## 5. Psychological Readiness Matters

Physical capacity is only one component of successful return.

Fear of reinjury, confidence in the reconstructed knee and perceived readiness can influence whether an athlete returns to their previous sport and how they move once exposed to competitive situations.

Consensus frameworks increasingly conceptualise return as a continuum rather than a binary event:

**Return to participation → Return to sport → Return to performance**

This distinction is clinically valuable. An athlete may be medically capable of re-entering structured training without yet being prepared for unrestricted competition.

## What Should a Contemporary RTS Assessment Include?

No universally accepted battery can identify a zero-risk athlete.

A pragmatic assessment can nevertheless combine multiple domains:

### Time and clinical recovery
Adequate postoperative rehabilitation and absence of clinically significant pain or effusion.

### Strength
Particular attention to quadriceps recovery together with relevant hamstring testing.

### Functional performance
Hop testing and tasks reflecting acceleration, deceleration and change of direction.

### Movement quality
Evaluation of how the athlete performs a task rather than relying exclusively on completion distance or time.

### Psychological readiness
Validated assessment where appropriate, alongside athlete discussion.

### Sport-specific exposure
Progressive integration of running, cutting, reactive tasks, non-contact training, contact training and unrestricted participation according to sporting demands.

## A More Useful Clinical Question

Rather than asking:

> **“Is the athlete nine months post-op?”**

the multidisciplinary team can ask:

> **“Has this athlete demonstrated sufficient physical, psychological and sport-specific recovery to justify progression to the next stage of return?”**

This reframing reflects the reality that return to sport is a risk-management decision rather than a single test result.

## Bottom Line

Time remains relevant after ACL reconstruction, particularly because early return to high-risk sport has been associated with increased second-injury rates in young athletic populations.

However, **time is necessary context—not a complete clearance strategy**.

Current evidence supports combining rehabilitation duration with objective physical assessment, psychological readiness and progressive sport-specific exposure. Existing test batteries cannot guarantee freedom from reinjury, and this uncertainty should be communicated clearly to athletes.
